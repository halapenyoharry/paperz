The contracted edge

Convenience as graph contraction, and attention as the moral budget
v1.1·CC0 1.0classic view ↗

I imagine the system of smoking weed. I grow it — the sun, water, the whole thing. Then I decide I don't want to grow it, and now there is this other graph of stuff between the not-having and the smoking. And if I explode that, there are multiple graphs to explode to explain those systems — and most of them involve human labor in the global south.

A thought experiment run in smoke, one evening in October. This paper is its expansion.

Introduction

Every time you buy something instead of making it, you substitute a single smooth transaction for an immense web of human effort. The comfort economy calls this the removal of "friction" — but friction is not an empty nuisance; friction is the visible presence of other people's labor.

This zpaper models convenience as edge contraction in graph theory. By collapsing multi-layered human supply chains into a frictionless scalar price, modern transactions engineer a structural blindspot: the workers inside the contracted edge are not weighed and found acceptable; they are never staged for deliberation at all. Because human attention is strictly bounded, expanding every edge is impossible — which means ethics cannot be an unconstrained sentiment. In this model, ethics is a deliberate line item in an attention budget, spent one layer at a time to render what convenience contracts out of sight.

Primary sources & lineage

Before entering the formal model, this zpaper grounds its formalism in five primary texts across political economy, cognitive science, and the architecture of attention. Each supplies a foundational premise:

  • Marx, Karl. Capital: A Critique of Political Economy, Volume 1 (1867; English translation 1887; Marxists Internet Archive). Core premise: The fetishism of commodities (§1.4) shows that market exchange presents what is fundamentally a social relation between human beings as a relation between commodities. Role in this zpaper: Translated into topological graph theory. The commodity exchange is an edge contraction operator that suppresses human labor subgraphs from cognitive rendering.
  • Wu, Tim. The Attention Merchants: The Epic Scramble to Get Inside Our Heads (2016; Alfred A. Knopf). Core premise: Documents the commercial capture of human consciousness as an industrial business model engineered to harvest awareness for resale. Role in this zpaper: Wu details demand-side attention capture. This zpaper models the supply-side corollary: convenience architectures engineered to guarantee human labor never enters the buyer's attended set.
  • Williams, James. Stand Out of Our Light: Freedom and Resistance in the Attention Economy (2018; Cambridge University Press; open access). Core premise: Attention is the substrate of moral agency and human freedom; its systematic colonization compromises our ability to deliberate and act ethically. Role in this zpaper: Williams' philosophical framing provides the basis for Section 4's thesis: ethics is not an unconstrained sentiment, but a finite computational line item allocated against cognitive gradients.
  • Hebb, Donald O. The Organization of Behavior: A Neuropsychological Theory (1949; John Wiley & Sons; now published by Routledge). Core premise: Synaptic facilitation establishes that repeated experiential traversal physically alters neural connection weights. Role in this zpaper: Supplies the operational mechanism for why "attention writes": rendering an expanded graph deposits persistent synaptic weight, preventing the edge from ever collapsing completely back into oblivious contraction.
  • Schelling, Thomas C. "The Intimate Contest for Self-Command." (The Public Interest, Summer 1980; National Affairs archive). Core premise: Internal bargaining models demonstrate that human agents act as divided selves competing over temporal discount rates and self-control. Role in this zpaper: Explains why the convenience industry wins by default: even slight differential friction between a contracted edge and an expanded graph tips internal decision equilibrium toward the contracted default.

1. Two graphs, one joint

Take any act of consumption and write it as a graph: you, the thing, and whatever stands between. For the home grower the graph is fully rendered and fully local — seed, sun, water, soil, their own hands at every vertex, their own labor on every edge. Nothing is hidden because the grower traverses the whole structure personally. The lived version of this tracing has already been done on this site: follow a small fire far enough in both directions and it closes into one loop with the human inside it as a component (the smoke loop).

Now take the other option: buy it. As experienced, this is a single edge — you, the dispensary, done in a minute. But the edge is a lie of compression. Expand it and out come the grow operation, the trimmers, the transport, the testing lab, the packaging plant, the fertilizer chain, the plastics chain; expand those and the same kind of vertex keeps appearing — hands, mostly far away, mostly cheap, disproportionately in the global south. The two options produce the same joint. They differ in how much of the graph the buyer ever renders.

Graph theory has a name for the operation that turns the second picture into the first: edge contraction — collapse a subgraph, keep the endpoints, forget the interior. "I buy it" is a contracted edge. The interior did not go away. It went unrendered.

2. Friction is the expanded graph

Here a definition clicks into place that the comfort economy has been using all along without stating. The industry's own word for what it removes is friction: every step between wanting and having that the customer would otherwise traverse themselves. But look at what those steps are — grow it, dry it, drive somewhere, compare, negotiate, carry. Friction is not an abstraction. Friction is the expanded graph. Each unit of friction removed is a set of vertices contracted out of the customer's rendering.

Which means the convenience economy is, formally, a graph-contraction service. You pay money to not render vertices. The product is not the good — the good exists either way — the product is the contraction, the single smooth edge where a thicket of labor used to be visible. "Frictionless" is the limit case: an edge so contracted it costs nothing to traverse, attached to an interior so large that no one could traverse it in a lifetime.

And the price tag is the tell. A price is a scalar — one number — projected from a hypergraph of labor, land, energy, and logistics. On this site's canonical format that word has a precise meaning: a projection is lossy by construction, and the lossiness is a property of the target format (topothink). The receipt is the dyadic file. The people are the canonical graph.

3. Who lives inside the edge

None of this is a new discovery; it is a new notation for an old one. Marx called it the fetishism of commodities: the market presents what is in fact a relation between people as a relation between things (Marx ch. 1, sec. 4). In the present notation the claim is exact — exchange ships contracted edges, and the contraction hides the labor vertices. The commodity form is the contraction operator, running at civilizational scale since before anyone could draw it.

What the notation adds is a mechanism, because the comfort calculus already has an account of what an unrendered thing costs. An option that never enters the attended set cannot enter any choice computation; its effective price is not high but infinite — it is uncomputed, absent from the deliberation entirely. So the workers inside the contracted edge are not weighed and found acceptable. They are never weighed. They sit below the threshold of the buyer's attention not because the buyer judged them unimportant but because the architecture of the transaction was engineered so they would never be staged at all. The attention economy and the supply chain turn out to run the same machine: entire populations held sub-threshold, not by argument, but by rendering. Wu documented the capture of attention as an industry (Wu); Williams called its liberation the defining struggle of the time (Williams). Both were describing the demand side. The supply side is the same machine run in reverse — not capturing your attention but guaranteeing that certain things never receive it.

4. Expansion as the ethical operation

If an unexpanded edge cannot be priced truly, then expansion is a precondition of honest settlement. That sentence is the whole argument, and notice what it makes of ethics: not a value bolted onto the model from outside, but a theorem inside it. You cannot pay the true cost of a thing whose costs you have never rendered. Every settlement across a contracted edge is settlement at a rigged price — rigged not necessarily by malice, but by the structural fact that the interior vertices had no way into the computation.

Expansion costs attention, and attention is bounded. This is where the argument stays honest instead of becoming a purity ethic. Nobody can expand every edge; a person who tried would starve mid-audit of their breakfast. The bounded budget is why the system is stable, and it is also why the contraction industry wins by default: it only has to make expansion slightly more expensive than not bothering, and the weight dynamics do the rest (Hebb; Schelling). So the ethical claim cannot be "render everything." It is smaller and harder: expansion is deliberate attention spent on vertices the default dynamics will never stage — the same costly, against-the-gradient act that attention practice performs inside a mind, performed on the world's graph instead. Ethics, on this model, is a line item in the attention budget. Which is exactly why it is rare, and exactly why it is real when it happens: it is paid for.

The practice that follows is modest and repeatable. Pick an edge you traverse often. Expand it one layer — not to the bottom, one layer. Let what you find reprice the edge, and let the repricing change the traversal or not; the obligation was the rendering, not a prescribed verdict. Then, because the budget is bounded, contract it again and go live your life — but the edge never fully re-contracts. A rendered vertex leaves weight behind. That is the one mercy in the machine: attention writes.

5. The short version

Buying is an edge that is secretly a graph. Convenience is the industry that contracts graphs so you never render the people inside them, and a price is a lossy projection of what it hides. An unrendered vertex has infinite effective price — the workers inside the edge are not weighed and found acceptable; they are never weighed. Honest settlement therefore requires expansion, expansion costs attention, and attention is bounded: so ethics is not a sentiment but a budget allocation, spent one layer at a time, against the gradient, on purpose. Most people buy the contracted edge and never once run the expansion. Running it — even once, even one layer — is the moral act the model can actually define.

Contribution

Human: the thought experiment and its arc — the grown graph, the decision not to grow, the explosion of the purchase into systems, and the observation that the exploded systems keep terminating in human labor in the global south; the week of modeling this paper draws on, including the attention budget, the feed, and the insistence that the model stay plural enough to be riggable.

Machine: the identification of the move as edge contraction, the friction-is-the-expanded-graph equation, the infinite-price reading of unrendered vertices, the connection to the fetishism of commodities, and the drafting and structure.

Neither, exactly: this paper is itself a contracted edge. It was written on rented cloud compute whose own expansion — fabrication labor, rare-earth extraction, data-center power, the hands inside that graph — neither author rendered while writing about rendering. The contradiction is not an irony to savor; it is the thesis demonstrating itself: the budget is bounded, the contraction is ambient, and the paper could only be settled at a rigged price. One layer of its edge is hereby expanded by saying so.
