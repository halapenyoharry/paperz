The smoke loop

A full-circuit model of a small fire — and the engine that reroutes its charge
v1.0·CC0 1.0classic view ↗

Someone lights a small fire — flower in a bowl, a rolled paper, a leaf wrap — and the ordinary description says a person is consuming a substance. Subject, verb, object: a user, a using, a thing used up.

This paper starts by refusing that grammar. Trace the circuit upstream past the material, and downstream past the exhale, and the picture inverts. There is no endpoint anywhere in it. There is one closed loop, and the human sits inside it as a component — the pump, in fact — not at its terminus.

The first half builds that model, because it is worth having for its own sake. The second half commits the move that makes models worth building at all: a loop you learn to see in one system starts showing up in others. It gets turned on something closer to home — on emotion itself, and then on the charge that a productive life accumulates and the question of where that charge discharges.

1. The loop

Energy enters as photons, eight minutes off the sun. The leaf fixes atmospheric carbon with that light, and everything that will later burn is stored sunlight: combustion is photosynthesis run backward, delayed by a season. In the glandular trichomes the plant runs a secondary chemistry — cannabinoid acids and terpenes, built as ultraviolet screen and herbivore deterrent. That these molecules fit a signaling system in vertebrate nervous tissue is not design in either direction; it is two independently evolved chemistries sharing a binding geometry, discovered by humans and then selected for, hard, across centuries. The human enters the loop upstream of the lighter: cultivation, harvest, drying, curing. Time itself is a component.

The apparatus is the coupling between fire and breath, and its topology decides what rides along. A water piece is a two-port pressure vessel: the hot aerosol breaks into bubbles below the waterline, each bubble a packet of smoke wrapped in a water interface, and the combined surface area dumps heat from combustion temperature to nearly room temperature in the length of a downstem. A paper is a traveling combustion front inside a consumable vessel — no cooling stage, and a sidestream port venting to the room between draws. A tobacco-leaf wrap is not a material swap but a topology change: a second plant, a second pharmacology, nicotine working its own receptor class alongside the first — two coupled drug loops sharing one airway.

The ember is the phase boundary, and the decisive chemistry happens in the millimeter behind it. The acid form the plant actually made sheds a carboxyl group under heat and becomes the active molecule. This deserves its own sentence: the plant never contained the drug in final form. It stored a precursor, and the fire performs the last synthetic step in the instant before inhalation. The lighter is a participant in biosynthesis.

The same fire opens the cost channel, and the model refuses to romanticize it away: carbon monoxide, particulates, pyrolysis byproducts, all traveling in the same aerosol, and no apparatus downstream strips the gas phase. An honest loop drawing keeps that channel visible.

Downstream is pure mechanics first. The diaphragm drops, pressure falls, and that single differential drives the entire external circuit — the smoker's own body powers the combustion that feeds it, a bellows and a beneficiary in one organ. The alveolar membrane is about half a micrometer of tissue with tens of square meters of area; the lipophilic molecule crosses in seconds, and the lung turns out to be a direct shunt into arterial blood. The brain is roughly twenty seconds downstream of the draw.

The receiving system predates the plant's arrival in human life by hundreds of millions of years, and its architecture runs backward: the downstream neuron, when strongly driven, releases a signal upstream across the synapse — a brief, local, made-on-demand whisper that says enough. The smoked molecule enters as a tonic broadcast on that phasic channel: not a new signal but a changed temporal structure of an old one, held open for an hour on a system built for milliseconds. The felt result maps onto receptor geography — memory's grip shortens where the hippocampus is dense, time stretches, associations connect at longer range than their usual routing permits. And one structural point the model insists on: the mind that notices all this is implemented in the same tissue being modulated. The observer is a component of the loop it observes.

Then the return. The exhale is mostly spent carrier — the transfer already happened in the first seconds — and its carbon dioxide diffuses out to the stomata of every plant downwind. The ash is the mineral fraction fire cannot touch, borrowed soil returnable to soil. The molecule itself leaves transformed through the liver over days. Carbon to air, mineral to ground, metabolite to water: the loop closes at planetary scale, and nothing in the drawing is a terminus.

2. Information dissolved to do something

Hold that model and look at emotion, because the nervous system runs two signaling layers and the loop above already touched both.

One layer is wires: synaptic, fast, point-to-point, addressed. This neuron tells that neuron. The other layer is water: neuromodulators, hormones, the endocannabinoids themselves — released into the bath, propagating by diffusion and circulation, slow, global, graded, and lingering past their trigger. Emotion rides the water layer, and that is not incidental to its function. It is the function.

An emotion is not a message sent somewhere. It is information dissolved into a medium — a broadcast reconfiguration of the whole system's operating parameters. Gain rises here, thresholds drop there, heart and gut and pupils and attention shift together, because the signal is in the solvent everything is sitting in. And it is information in the control sense, not the descriptive sense: not a report about the world but an imperative about readiness. Fear is a body pre-configured for a family of actions before any action is chosen.

So the compact formula: an emotion is a compressed appraisal, broadcast as a whole-system parameter shift, biased toward a family of actions, implemented in a medium slow enough to outlast its cause. The lingering is why mood exists at all — a wire signal would be over in milliseconds; the chemical one decays on the timescale situations actually unfold on.

One more loop hides in it. The feeling of an emotion is plausibly the system reading its own wet state back: brain sets the body's parameters, the altered body is sensed, and the sensing is the feeling. You do not have emotions so much as overhear your own broadcast. The observer inside the loop, again — the pattern this site's larger program keeps finding wherever information takes shape (topothink).

3. The misaddressed urge

Now a worked example, reported in first person because it ran live.

Momentum, for me, is built from repeated productive sessions, and those sessions accumulate charge — real, chemical, dissolved charge. Hours at the screens generate arousal, and arousal arrives unlabeled. It is propulsion looking for an address, and habit supplies cached ones. At 11:17 on a recent Sunday night, mid-flow, mine supplied the usual address: a venue engineered precisely for transducing accumulated charge into spending, open late, no agenda, reputation preceding me. I have renamed that place "the gravity well where hopes go to get transduced to others' disappointments" — a name built deliberately to tell the signal to stop.

The name works, partly, and it is worth being exact about why. Putting an affect into words measurably dampens the broadcast — wire-layer work against water-layer signal. And this particular name does something sharper: it pre-runs the appraisal all the way to the outcome, dragging the stalled days that follow such a night forward into the present tense, so the decision gets made with the whole timeline in frame instead of just the next hour.

It also only half-works, and the reason is the asymmetry between the layers. Words are fast and thin; chemistry is slow and thick. A sentence can veto a routing. It cannot un-dissolve a signal. What finishes the job belongs to the wet layer itself: movement metabolizes what language can only refuse. That night it was yoga — the charge circulated instead of accumulating against a closed door, and came out the other side as usable energy.

And there is a third move, the one the rest of this paper is about. The urge was never about the venue. The signal might have been saying connect, or only spend this charge; the gravity well was a routing, not the message. A routing can be rewritten.

4. The engine

Suppression loses the energy. Transduction keeps it.

The rewrite is a redirect: the charge the body addressed to the night out gets readdressed to the desk, and the work it produces generates the next session's charge. Creation, accumulation, reroute, creation — a loop around the loops, running on the same propulsion that used to leave through the cached address.

Control theory says when this works. In the smoke model, the smoked route is steerable because feedback is fast — sense the state, adjust, stop — while the oral route overshoots because an hour of dead time sits in the loop, and a long delay in a feedback path is the textbook condition for instability. Life-routing obeys the same law. A reroute holds when its reward arrives fast enough to be felt as reward; a reroute framed as restriction, with the payoff deferred past the signal's decay time, is an edible — it will be overridden before it reports back. The goal is not to make the discharge forbidden but to make the alternative address pay on the signal's own timescale, so that not-spending becomes as effortless as spending ever was.

And the engine does not require a destination. The quest for purpose being unending is not the engine's failure mode; it is its fuel condition. The loop in section 1 has no terminus and does not need one — the circuit is the thing. One can stop demanding that the wheel arrive somewhere and still push it with full commitment, which is an old resolution to an old problem, and the only one that keeps the energy in the system.

5. Make it again

One made thing is an event. That is fine; the world is full of events.

But the same thing made again — after learning where its edges are, where the material pushed back, which parts were load-bearing and which were decoration — and then made again after that, is a different category of thing. Iteration converts output into instrument. The edges you learned become the next version's starting constraints; the mistakes stop being losses and become the map. Somewhere in the repetition the center of gravity moves: you are no longer a person who made a thing, you are a system that makes, and the thing is one of its passes. This site's first paper argued that what stays invariant under deformation is the part worth teaching first (squash the triangle); iteration is how a maker finds the invariants of their own work — what survives every version is what the work actually is.

Mastery of systems, then, is not control of a system from outside. It is becoming a reliable component of your own loops: knowing your signals' real addresses, owning the reroutes, keeping the cost channels drawn on the diagram instead of off it, and running the engine through enough passes that the passes themselves compound. I am not claiming that mastery. I am striving at it — and the striving, readdressed nightly from the gravity well to the desk, is the engine running.

6. The short version

A small fire, traced far enough, is one loop with you inside it as the pump. An emotion is information dissolved into a body to do something. A charge with a bad address can be named, metabolized, or rerouted — and rerouted into making, it becomes an engine. Making one thing is fine. Making it again, knowing its edges, and again after that, is something else entirely.
