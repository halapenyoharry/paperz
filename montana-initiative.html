Powers, not rights

Montana's Initiative 194 and the chain of cases it has to get past

Just want people to have the full story. because this particular issue is the biggest and worth the detail every time.

The first line is a reply typed under a post celebrating Montana's vote, with the case names left blank to be filled in. The second is the instruction that followed. This paper fills in the blanks and then keeps going, because the full story has a Montana case in it that the celebration left out.

Introduction

On November 3, 2026, Montana voters approved Initiative 194, which its backers call the Montana Plan, by roughly 65 percent to 35 percent (Wikipedia, "2026 Montana Initiative 194"; Montana Free Press). The measure changes state law so that "artificial persons," a category that covers corporations, nonprofits, trusts, partnerships, trade associations and unincorporated associations doing business in the state, no longer hold the power to contribute anything of value to candidate elections, to support or oppose political parties, or to support or oppose ballot issues. An entity that does so forfeits its privileges to do business in Montana (Wikipedia). It takes effect January 1, 2027. Less than forty-eight hours after the polls closed, the Institute for Free Speech filed suit in the United States District Court for the District of Montana on behalf of the Frontier Institute, the Montana Family Foundation and the Montana Republican State Central Committee, asking the court to declare the law "unconstitutionally void and unenforceable" (Reason; Flathead Beacon).

The online reaction treated the vote as a direct answer to Citizens United. That is half right. Citizens United is one node in a chain of decisions that runs from 1976 to June of this year, and the chain has a Montana link already: in 2012 the Supreme Court took Montana's 1912 Corrupt Practices Act, a corporate spending ban passed by citizen initiative in response to the copper barons, and reversed the Montana Supreme Court's decision upholding it in a per curiam opinion of a few paragraphs, without oral argument (American Tradition Partnership v. Bullock). Anyone predicting what happens to Initiative 194 has to start there.

The measure's authors know this. Their claim is not that the chain is wrong. Their claim is that the chain is about the wrong thing. Every case in it asks what a government may do to a speaker's right. Initiative 194 is written to ask what a state must grant a creature of its own law by way of powers. If a court accepts that the question has changed, the chain does not bind. If a court decides the new question is the old question in a different coat, Montana has been here before and knows how it ends. This paper lays out both the chain and the switch, as structure.

Primary sources & lineage

Before entering the model, this zpaper grounds itself in eight primary texts, seven of them judicial opinions and one a two-hundred-year-old case about a college. Each supplies a premise.

  • Supreme Court of the United States. Buckley v. Valeo (1976; 424 U.S. 1). Core premise: A limit on political spending "necessarily reduces the quantity of expression," so expenditure limits get the strictest review. Contribution limits are a lesser burden and may stand to prevent corruption or its appearance. The interest in equalizing the influence of rich and poor is rejected as a justification. Role in this zpaper: The root node. Every later case inherits the contribution-versus-expenditure split and the corruption-only rationale.
  • Supreme Court of the United States. First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti (1978; 435 U.S. 765). Core premise: Massachusetts could not forbid corporations from spending to influence a ballot referendum. The speech is protected because of what it says, not who says it. Role in this zpaper: The first corporate-speaker case, and the one that reaches ballot measures, which Initiative 194 also covers.
  • Supreme Court of the United States. Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission (2010; 558 U.S. 310). Core premise: "The Government may not suppress political speech based on the speaker's corporate identity." Independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption." Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce (1990) is overruled, and the part of McConnell v. FEC (2003) that relied on it. Disclosure requirements survive. Role in this zpaper: The node everyone names. The paper's question is whether Initiative 194 attaches to it at all.
  • United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, en banc. SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission (2010; 599 F.3d 686). Core premise: If independent spending cannot corrupt, then contributions to a group that only spends independently cannot corrupt either, so limits on those contributions fall. Role in this zpaper: The lower-court link in the chain, decided two months after Citizens United, which is what actually created the super PAC.
  • Supreme Court of the United States. American Tradition Partnership, Inc. v. Bullock (2012; 567 U.S. 516). Core premise: Asked whether Citizens United applies to Montana's 1912 ban on corporate expenditures, the Court answered, "There can be no serious doubt that it does," and reversed the Montana Supreme Court summarily. Four justices dissented. Role in this zpaper: The Montana link. The paper's central claim is that this case, not Citizens United, is the one Initiative 194 must be read against.
  • Supreme Court of Montana. Western Tradition Partnership, Inc. v. Attorney General (2011; 2011 MT 328). Core premise: Chief Justice McGrath's opinion upheld the 1912 act, holding that Montana's history of corporate capture gave the state a compelling interest that Citizens United's record had not addressed. Role in this zpaper: The argument that lost in 2012, preserved so it can be compared with the argument Montana is making now.
  • Supreme Court of the United States. McCutcheon v. Federal Election Commission (2014; 572 U.S. 185). Core premise: Aggregate limits on one donor's total giving per cycle are invalid. The only corruption a government may fight is quid pro quo: "Ingratiation and access . . . are not corruption." Role in this zpaper: Shows the chain still moving after the Montana reversal, and narrows the corruption interest Montana would otherwise invoke.
  • Supreme Court of the United States. Trustees of Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819; 17 U.S. 518). Core premise: A corporation is "an artificial being, invisible, intangible, and existing only in contemplation of law," possessing only the properties its charter confers. The decision protected the college's charter as a contract, and states responded by reserving in every charter the power to alter, amend or repeal it. Role in this zpaper: The source of the phrase "artificial persons" and of the reserved-powers doctrine that Initiative 194's authors stand on.

1. The blanks, filled

The reply that started this paper had four blanks. Here they are, in the order the law built them.

Citizens United v. FEC (2010). A nonprofit corporation wanted to distribute a film attacking a presidential candidate inside the window where federal law barred corporate electioneering. The Court, five to four in Justice Kennedy's opinion, held that the ban on corporate independent expenditures violated the First Amendment. It overruled Austin, which had allowed states to counter "the corrosive and distorting effects of immense aggregations of wealth" held in corporate form, and it held that independent spending, being uncoordinated with a candidate, cannot corrupt (Citizens United, syllabus).

The lower court: SpeechNow.org v. FEC (D.C. Cir. 2010). Two months later, the D.C. Circuit sitting en banc took the premise and ran it one step. If independent expenditures cannot corrupt, then there is no anti-corruption interest in capping what a person gives to a group that makes only independent expenditures. The federal contribution limits fell as applied to such groups (SpeechNow; Institute for Free Speech brief, citing 599 F.3d at 696). The Supreme Court never heard this case. It is the law of one circuit, treated as binding ever since, and it is what the phrase "super PAC" refers to.

McCutcheon v. FEC (2014). Federal law capped not only what a donor could give to each candidate but the total a donor could give to all candidates and parties in a cycle. A plurality led by Chief Justice Roberts struck the aggregate caps, and in doing so narrowed the only interest the government may assert to quid pro quo exchange (McCutcheon).

What allowed what. Citizens United allowed the corporate treasury into independent spending. SpeechNow allowed unlimited money into the vehicles that do that spending. McCutcheon removed the ceiling on how many candidates one donor could reach. And in June 2026, NRSC v. FEC struck the limits on what parties may spend in coordination with their own candidates, overruling a 2001 precedent (Skadden, "NRSC v. FEC"). The chain is still being extended.

That is the usual list. It is missing the case that matters most for Montana.

2. The Montana link

In 1912, three years after the Senate had refused to seat William A. Clark over the purchased legislature that elected him, Montana voters passed the Corrupt Practices Act by initiative, three to one. It barred corporate contributions to and expenditures on candidate elections. A recent Daily Montanan essay argues the target was less the copper kings as men than the Amalgamated Copper Company, which by then owned the mines, the smelters, the sawmills and most of the state's daily newspapers (Daily Montanan, "It Was the Corporations"). The ban stood for a century.

After Citizens United, a group then called Western Tradition Partnership sued to have it struck. A district judge agreed. On December 30, 2011, the Montana Supreme Court reversed, five to two, in an opinion by Chief Justice McGrath holding that Montana's documented history of corporate control gave it a compelling interest that the federal record in Citizens United had not contained (Western Tradition Partnership; Wikipedia, "American Tradition Partnership v. Bullock"). One of the two dissenters agreed with every word about corruption and dissented only because a state court may not refuse to follow the Supreme Court.

He was right about the mechanics. On June 25, 2012, the Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed in the same order, per curiam, without briefing on the merits or argument. The whole opinion fits on a page. The question was whether Citizens United applies to the Montana statute, and the answer was: "There can be no serious doubt that it does." Montana's arguments "either were already rejected in Citizens United, or fail to meaningfully distinguish that case." Justice Breyer, for four justices, wrote that Montana's experience "casts grave doubt on the Court's supposition that independent expenditures do not corrupt," and then voted to deny review anyway, because with five votes against him he saw no "significant possibility of reconsideration" (American Tradition Partnership v. Bullock).

Read the structure of that. The state court's argument was our history is different. The response was that history does not change the rule, because the rule is about the speaker's right, and the right does not vary by state. A more vivid record of corruption was not a distinction; it was an invitation to relitigate Citizens United, declined.

That is the precedent Initiative 194 is actually filed under. Not Citizens United in the abstract, but Citizens United as already applied to this state, to a corporate spending ban passed by these voters' great-grandparents, with the summary reversal signaling that the Court considered the question closed.

3. The switch: from rights to powers

Everything in Figure 1 lives in one body of law. Each case asks: given a speaker with a First Amendment right, what may a government do to it? Montana's 2011 argument stayed inside that frame and offered a better reason for restricting the right. It lost because better reasons were not the issue.

Initiative 194 does not offer a reason. It changes the subject of the sentence. The operative ballot language is that the act "limits the powers of artificial persons to those powers necessary or convenient as provided by law," and election spending is not among them (Wikipedia). The theory, laid out by Tom Moore of the Center for American Progress in a 2025 white paper and taken up by Jeff Mangan, Montana's former Commissioner of Political Practices, who founded the Transparent Election Initiative to carry it (High Country News; Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance), goes like this:

A corporation has no powers except those its chartering state grants. This is Marshall in Dartmouth College: "an artificial being, invisible, intangible, and existing only in contemplation of law," possessing "only those properties which the charter of its creation confers upon it" (Dartmouth College v. Woodward). After that decision made charters into contracts, every state wrote a reservation into its corporate code: the power to alter, amend or repeal.

Citizens United addressed a corporation that already had the power to spend. Federal law had tried to restrict a power the corporations in question possessed under their state charters. The case decided that Congress could not restrict it. Moore's argument is that it never decided a state had to confer it in the first place (Center for American Progress).

So withdraw the power. Rewrite the corporate code so that no artificial person chartered or doing business in Montana has the power to spend in elections. Natural persons keep every political right they have. Political committees stay governed by campaign finance law. A super PAC may still spend, but only with money that came from identifiable human beings (Center for American Progress). The sponsors' line is that they are not trying to overturn Citizens United, only to make it "irrelevant" (Yahoo News / The Conversation).

In graph terms: the 2011 argument tried to add weight to an edge inside the First Amendment subgraph. The 2026 argument tries to move the whole question to a different subgraph, corporate law, where the state is sovereign and the precedents are Dartmouth and Bank of Augusta v. Earle (1839), which holds that a corporation operates in a state other than its own only by that state's comity and on its terms (Bank of Augusta v. Earle). If the move is allowed, none of Figure 1 applies, because none of it is about what a charter must contain.

Hawaii made the same move first by statute. Senate Bill 2471, signed by Governor Green in May 2026 as Act 11, specifies that the powers of Hawaii-chartered entities do not include spending or contributing to influence elections or ballot measures (Spectrum News Hawaii; Honolulu Civil Beat). Montana is the first to do it by popular vote, and as of September, bills modeled on the plan had been filed in more than thirty states (Wikipedia).

4. Where the switch can fail

The plaintiffs' complaint does not engage the powers theory on its own terms. It asserts that Initiative 194 "attempts to accomplish indirectly what the Constitution expressly forbids: silencing organizations that engage in political advocacy," and that it "denies the individuals constituting these groups, especially those not in the majority, the ability to pool their resources" (Reason). Bradley Smith, a former FEC chairman, put the objection in one sentence: a state cannot sidestep the First Amendment by calling political spending a corporate power instead of a right (Fortune). There are three distinct ways a court could agree with him, and they are worth separating because they fail at different points in the structure.

Failure point one: the unconstitutional-conditions doctrine. A government may not condition a benefit it controls on the surrender of a constitutional right. The state controls the corporate charter; the measure says you may have one only if you do not spend in politics; the pre-election challengers argued exactly that the measure "improperly makes entities' benefits depend on giving up First Amendment rights" (Daily Montanan, April 1, 2026). This is the sharpest edge back from the corporate-law subgraph into the First Amendment one, and it is the argument the sponsors' public material addresses least directly. Their answer has to be that the charter is not a benefit conditioned on silence but a definition of what the entity is, and that there was never a rights-holder to condition until the state created one. Whether a court buys that distinction is, in the end, the case.

Failure point two: the speaker-identity rule. Citizens United says the government may not suppress political speech "based on the speaker's corporate identity." A court could hold that a law whose only trigger is corporate identity is that suppression whatever vocabulary it uses, and that Bellotti settled it for ballot measures in 1978. On this reading Initiative 194 is the 1912 act in new words, and American Tradition Partnership controls: "no serious doubt."

Failure point three: the scope. The definition reaches nonprofits, unions and unincorporated associations, not just business corporations, and the penalty is forfeiture of the privilege to do business in Montana at all. The pre-election suit called the measure unclear; the federal complaint calls it overbroad and vague (Flathead Beacon). A court that did not want to reach the big question could rule here, and a court that wanted to protect the big question for a better case might prefer to.

The powers theory survives only if it survives all three. Figure 2 draws the two frames and the bridge between them.

5. Who is where, and what each can do

This section is the veto-player picture, in the manner of three gates.

The federal district court in Helena has the first move. The measure takes effect January 1, 2027, so a preliminary injunction motion is likely before then. A district judge bound by American Tradition Partnership has an easy route to an injunction if the judge reads the measure as a spending ban. The powers theory asks the judge to read it as something the Supreme Court has not addressed, which a district court can do but rarely does when a summary reversal of the same state sits on the shelf.

The Ninth Circuit hears the appeal either way. The Supreme Court decides whether to take it. The Court that decided ATP v. Bullock five to four in 2012 has since decided McCutcheon and NRSC, each extending the chain. Nothing in its recent record suggests appetite for narrowing Citizens United. The sponsors' bet is not on that appetite. It is on the Court agreeing that the question is a corporate-law question, which is a bet on doctrine, not on votes.

The Montana Legislature can repeal a statutory initiative. Initiative 194 was drafted as a constitutional initiative and became statutory only after the attorney general's single-subject objection to the constitutional version was upheld in January 2026 (Wikipedia). A Republican legislature meets in January 2027. Repealing a measure that passed with 65 percent, including, on the arithmetic, a large share of Republican voters, is a political cost; it is not a legal obstacle (High Country News).

The secretary of state and the attorney general, both named as defendants, both opponents of the measure, control the pace and vigor of its defense. The attorney general's office said before the vote that the measure could cause "significant material harm" to businesses (Wikipedia). Whether the state defends its own law with conviction, or whether the Transparent Election Initiative has to intervene to do it, is an open question with precedent on both sides.

Montana voters have spoken and cannot speak again on this until 2028. The two-to-one margin, in a state Trump carried three times by double digits, is the fact every other actor is now pricing in.

6. What the 1912 act and the 2026 act have in common, and what they do not

Both were passed by citizen initiative, over the opposition of the state's business associations, in response to corporate money in state politics. Both bar corporate spending on candidates. Both were sued at once.

The 1912 act was a prohibition addressed to corporations as speakers. It said a corporation may not make an expenditure. The 2026 act is a definition addressed to the state's own corporate code. It says an artificial person does not have this power. The authors did not choose that grammar for elegance. They chose it because the first grammar lost in 2012 and the summary reversal told them precisely which grammar had lost.

That is the structural claim of this paper, and it cuts in both directions. If the grammar matters, Montana has found the one sentence the chain does not cover, and thirty states are watching. If the grammar does not matter, then what passed on November 3 is the 1912 act, and the per curiam opinion from 2012 is already the ruling.

8. The short version

Montana voters passed a corporate election spending ban in 1912, the Montana Supreme Court upheld it in 2011, and the United States Supreme Court erased it in 2012 in a one-page opinion that said there could be no serious doubt Citizens United applied. Montana voters have now passed a second one, by two to one, and were sued within two days. The difference is grammar: the old law told corporations what they could not do, and the new law tells the state's corporate code what powers an artificial person does not have. Citizens United, SpeechNow, McCutcheon and NRSC are all about the first grammar. Dartmouth College and the reservation clause in every state's corporate code are about the second. The lawsuit will try to drag the second back into the first by way of the unconstitutional-conditions doctrine. If it succeeds, the 2012 ruling is already the answer. If it fails, Montana found the sentence the chain does not reach, and Hawaii and thirty other states are standing behind it.
