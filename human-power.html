A physics notebook page from a world-building folder (physics_of_the_wheel.md), set down to test whether one human life of physical labor could fill a mechanical battery. As a casual check for a story, the note worked. Tested strictly as physics, checking one flywheel number exposed two silent breaks in the model.

Introduction

How much usable mechanical work does a single human body hold over a whole working life? Put a healthy adult on bicycle pedals at a steady, sustainable 100 watts for 12 hours every day over 40 years, and the total shaft output comes to 63.07 gigajoules (17,520 kilowatt-hours). Expressed in familiar fuels, forty years of hard daily labor equals roughly 10.5 barrels of crude oil or 497 gallons of gasoline (U.S. EIA).

Can you trap that lifetime of labor inside one spinning flywheel and hand the wheel forward at the end? The physical calculation reveals two unforgiving walls, plus an easy factor-of-ten slip in the starting draft. First, a 1,000-kilogram, 2-meter-radius rotor spun to 50,000 revolutions per minute stores 27.42 gigajoules, ten times more than the 2.7 gigajoules written in the notebook, yet its outer rim travels at 10.47 kilometers per second (Mach 30.5) and tears itself apart at roughly 58 times the tensile strength of carbon/epoxy composite (Genta; TorayCMA). Second, even an unrealistically quiet flywheel losing just 0.1 percent of its charge per hour hits a hard steady-state ceiling after roughly four months, flatlining at 50 kilowatt-hours (0.29 percent of 63.07 gigajoules), after which the remaining 39.7 years of pedaling do nothing except pay the leak.

Primary sources & lineage

Before walking through the numbers, this zpaper anchors each physical ceiling in eight primary sources across exercise physiology, mechanical flywheel design, aerospace composite data, and energy unit standards.

  • Wilkie, D. R. "Man as a Source of Mechanical Power." (1960; Ergonomics, vol. 3, no. 1, pp. 1-8). Core premise: Quantifies the duration-versus-power frontier for human muscle, contrasting multi-horsepower bursts lasting under a second against aerobic continuous mechanical limits on the order of 100 to 200 watts. Role in this zpaper: Grounds why 75 to 100 watts is a realistic steady physiological ceiling across long daily shifts rather than an arbitrary round number.
  • Wilson, David Gordon, and Theodor Schmidt. Bicycling Science (2020; 4th ed., The MIT Press). Core premise: Establishes bicycle transmission efficiency, human metabolic efficiency (roughly 20 to 25 percent from food chemical energy to mechanical crank work), and power-duration curves. Role in this zpaper: Converts 63.07 gigajoules of shaft work back into daily food calories and explains why biological maintenance dwarfs stored mechanical work.
  • Genta, Giancarlo. Kinetic Energy Storage: Theory and Practice of Advanced Flywheel Systems (1985; Butterworth-Heinemann). Core premise: Derives the structural limit of rotating rotors, showing that maximum specific energy obeys e_max = K (σ / ρ), where K is a dimensionless shape factor, σ is allowable working tensile stress, and ρ is density. Role in this zpaper: Supplies the exact thin-rim (K = 0.5) and uniform-disk (K = 0.606 for Poisson ratio ν = 0.3) burst equations driving Figure 1 and Section 2.
  • Bolund, Björn, Hans Bernhoff, and Mats Leijon. "Flywheel energy and power storage systems." (2007; Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews, vol. 11, no. 2, pp. 235-258). Core premise: Surveys modern vacuum-housed magnetic-bearing rotors alongside standby losses from eddy currents, residual aerodynamic windage, and power electronics. Role in this zpaper: Frames why fractional-percent hourly losses persist in real machines even without mechanical contact bearings.
  • Jensen, Ralph H., and Timothy P. Dever. G2 Flywheel Module Design (2006; NASA Glenn Research Center, NTRS 20060028492). Core premise: Documents a physical 60,000 RPM aerospace composite flywheel storing 525 watt-hours (1.89 megajoules) at 1 kilowatt continuous rating using magnetic bearings. Role in this zpaper: Demonstrates why 50,000 to 60,000 RPM requires a small compact radius (centimeters, not two meters) to keep rim velocity below composite failure.
  • Dever, Timothy P., et al. Assessment of Technologies for Noncryogenic Hybrid Electric Propulsion (2015; NASA Glenn Research Center, NTRS 20150000747). Core premise: Assesses system-level aerospace carbon-fiber flywheel specific energies (accounting for burst containment, magnetic bearings, and motor-generators) at roughly 25 to 50 watt-hours per kilogram. Role in this zpaper: Separates idealized filament energy density from full-module mass when sizing storage for gigajoule loads.
  • Toray Composite Materials America. TORAYCA T1000G Carbon Fiber Data Sheet (2024; TorayCMA). Core premise: Lists typical uniaxial tensile strength of 6,370 MPa and fiber density of 1.80 g/cm³ for commercial high-strength T1000G aerospace carbon fiber. Role in this zpaper: Anchors the upper composite burst curves in Figure 1 and Section 2 with real datasheet values rather than generic estimates.
  • U.S. Energy Information Administration. "Energy Units and Calculators Explained." (2026; EIA, with BTU reference). Core premise: Standardizes U.S. thermal energy conversion factors: 1 barrel of crude oil = 5.689 million Btu (6.002 GJ), 1 gallon of gasoline = 120,214 Btu (126.84 MJ), and 1 Btu = 1,055.06 J. 1. Forty years at the pedals

A single sustained hundred-watt shift looks deceptively modest, roughly the electrical draw of a bright incandescent bulb or an idle desktop workstation. Held for 12 hours a day, that output produces 4.32 megajoules (1.20 kilowatt-hours) each evening. Multiplied across 365 days a year for 40 years (14,600 days), the integral reaches 17,520 kilowatt-hours, or 63.072 gigajoules, matching the 63 GJ figure in the original notebook (Wilkie; Wilson and Schmidt).

Where that work becomes humbling is the metabolic denominator. Human skeletal muscle converts chemical food energy into crank work at a gross efficiency of roughly 22 to 25 percent (Wilson and Schmidt). At 24 percent efficiency, delivering 4.32 megajoules to the drivetrain burns 18.0 megajoules (4,302 kilocalories) of extra food on top of basal metabolism (~1,800 kilocalories/day), requiring roughly 6,100 kilocalories every single day for four decades. Even when captured cleanly at the shaft, 63.07 gigajoules equals just 10.51 barrels of crude oil or 497 gallons of gasoline (U.S. EIA).

Figure 2. Left: 40 years of continuous 100 W pedaling (12 hours/day) measured against EIA chemical fuel equivalents and 40 years of continuous 20 W human brain metabolism (25.23 GJ). Right: flywheel rotor mass required to hold all 63.07 GJ at 35 percent of thin-rim burst energy (~2.9x stress safety margin).

Compare that body-scale number to the brain inside the rider. As examined in the twenty-watt watch, the human brain runs continuously at roughly 20 watts, day and night, awake or asleep. Across those same 40 years, 20 continuous watts sums to 25.23 gigajoules, forty percent as much energy as 12 hours a day of hard leg labor, spent strictly maintaining neural order against entropy. And viewed through the contracted edge, modern convenience routinely hides that conversion factor: ten and a half barrels of crude oil compress an entire working lifetime of human muscle into the trunk volume of a compact car.

2. Two ways the draft flywheel breaks

The notebook tested storing that 63 GJ lifetime output in a carbon-fiber wheel with mass m = 1,000 kg and radius r = 2 m spinning at 50,000 RPM, estimating its stored energy at 2.7 GJ. Checking both the kinetic energy equation and the structural stress equation turns up two separate problems.

First, a straightforward arithmetic slip undercounted the energy by a factor of ten. At 50,000 RPM, angular velocity is ω = 50,000 × 2π / 60 = 5,236 rad/s. For a solid uniform disk (I = ½ m r² = 2,000 kg·m²), kinetic energy E = ½ I ω² equals 27.42 gigajoules (or 54.83 GJ for a pure thin-rim ring with I = m r²), not 2.7 GJ.

Second, and far more fatal, spinning a 2-meter radius wheel to 50,000 RPM pushes its outer rim to velocity v = ω r = 10,472 m/s (10.47 km/s, faster than low Earth orbital velocity, or Mach 30.5 in air). Click Test draft (1 t @ 50,000 RPM) in Figure 1 above and the wheel shatters immediately. Why? Because centrifugal hoop tension in a thin rotating ring equals σ_θ = ρ v², independent of rotor mass or radius (Genta). Rearranging gives a pure material ceiling for both rim speed and specific energy:

v_burst = √(σ / ρ) and e_max = E / m = K (σ / ρ)

Because kinetic energy scales with v² and stress also scales with v², making a wheel heavier or wider never raises its burst speed. For a thin rim (K = 0.5) wound from high-strength aerospace carbon/epoxy (60 percent Toray T1000G fiber by volume: composite hoop strength ~3,000 MPa, composite density ~1,580 kg/m³), v_burst = 1,378 m/s, limiting a 2-meter rotor to 6,579 RPM (TorayCMA). At 50,000 RPM, centrifugal stress reaches 173,266 MPa, roughly 58 times the composite's breaking limit. NASA Glenn's G2 flywheel achieves 60,000 RPM safely not by defying material limits, but by shrinking radius to centimeters rather than meters (Jensen and Dever).

To store all 63.07 gigajoules safely at 35 percent of thin-rim burst energy (~2.9x stress safety factor), you do not speed up a one-tonne wheel; you scale rotor mass up until specific energy fits inside the material ceiling:

  • Mild steel (A36, 250 MPa): bursts at 178 m/s (852 RPM at 2 m), holds 4 Wh/kg at burst, requiring 11,317 tonnes safely.
  • Alloy steel (4340, 1,100 MPa): bursts at 374 m/s (1,787 RPM at 2 m), holds 19 Wh/kg at burst, requiring 2,572 tonnes safely.
  • Titanium (Ti-6Al-4V, 900 MPa): bursts at 451 m/s (2,152 RPM at 2 m), holds 28 Wh/kg at burst, requiring 1,774 tonnes safely.
  • E-glass / epoxy (1,000 MPa): bursts at 707 m/s (3,376 RPM at 2 m), holds 69 Wh/kg at burst, requiring 721 tonnes safely.
  • Carbon / epoxy (T1000G 60%, 3,000 MPa): bursts at 1,378 m/s (6,579 RPM at 2 m), holds 264 Wh/kg at burst, requiring 190 tonnes safely.
  • Complete aerospace module (with housing, vacuum, bearings): NASA's ~25 to 50 Wh/kg system envelope puts full hardware mass near 350 to 700 tonnes (Dever et al.).

3. Why a fractional leak caps forty years at four months

Suppose you replace the one-tonne wheel with 190 tonnes of safe carbon-fiber rotor so nothing ever bursts. Now test the second assumption from the notebook: a vacuum-housed magnetic-bearing flywheel losing just 0.1 percent of its stored energy per hour (Bolund, Bernhoff, and Leijon).

Notice the structural difference between human input and standby drag. Pedaling adds energy at a fixed average rate, P_avg = 50 W (100 W averaged across 12 hours of work and 12 hours of rest). Friction and magnetic eddy currents drain energy at a rate proportional to how much energy is already in the wheel: P_loss = λ E, where λ = 0.001 / 3,600 s⁻¹ gives a characteristic time constant τ = 1 / λ = 41.7 days. Stored energy obeys the linear differential equation:

dE / dt = P_avg - λ E so E(t) = (P_avg / λ)(1 - e^(-λt))

The moment E(t) climbs high enough that 0.1 percent per hour equals 50 watts, net accumulation drops to zero permanently:

E_eq = P_avg / λ = 50 W × 3,600,000 s = 180 MJ = 50.0 kWh

Fifty kilowatt-hours is 0.29 percent of 63.07 gigajoules. Reaching 95 percent of that ceiling (3τ) takes just 125 days. From day 125 onward through year 40, the rider works 12 hours every single day solely to hold steady against standby leakage, much like the thermodynamic spinlock loops traced in the smoke loop and the twenty-watt watch.

Figure 3. Charge curves over 40 years of pedaling (P_avg = 50 W) across four standby loss rates. At 0.1%/hr, energy saturates at 50 kWh (0.18 GJ) within four months; even 100x tighter losses (0.001%/hr, an 11.4-year decay constant) still cap storage under 18 GJ.

Even cutting standby loss by a factor of ten (0.01 percent per hour, τ = 417 days) caps lifetime storage at 500 kilowatt-hours (1.80 GJ, or 2.85 percent of the pedaled work). Only ultra-low-loss cryogenic superconducting bearings holding leak rates near or below 0.0001 percent per hour (τ > 100 years) preserve most of a human lifetime's mechanical work in kinetic form. Over decades, the best mechanical "flywheel" is often a chemical battery or synthesized fuel tank, where standby self-discharge approaches zero per year rather than tenths of a percent per hour.

4. Honest scoping and the story boundary

None of these limits break the premise of storing work in spin for a poetic myth; in fact, knowing the physics sharpens what a story chooses to change. If the goal is literally to trap 40 years of pedaling, the wheel must either hold hundreds of tonnes of carbon rim or sit on virtually zero-loss superconducting bearings. If the loss rate stays at 0.1 percent per hour, stopping for sleep no longer drains a 63 GJ vault; rather, the vault never filled past 50 kilowatt-hours in the first place, which turns continuous pedaling into an even starker Sisyphus trap: every stroke keeps one tiny charge alive against immediate decay.

Checking the math also leaves the underlying fictional world and characters untouched. Real mechanics, textbook equations, Toray datasheet limits, and EIA fuel factors belong in the commons without revealing any plot, voice, or narrative craft built atop them.

5. The short version

Forty years of sustained 100-watt pedaling for 12 hours a day sums to 63.07 gigajoules (17,520 kilowatt-hours), the thermal energy of 10.5 barrels of crude oil or 497 gallons of gasoline. Trying to store that energy inside a 1,000-kilogram, 2-meter carbon flywheel at 50,000 RPM runs into a misread decimal (27.42 GJ, not 2.7 GJ) and 58 times the rotor's tensile breaking stress at Mach 30.5. Scaling safely to 190 tonnes of composite avoids bursting, yet at a 0.1 percent per hour standby leak the wheel still saturates at 50 kilowatt-hours in four months, turning the remaining 39.7 years of labor into pure replacement of heat.
